Chapter 1

The big picture

What the project is, why it exists and how the repository holds together. You learn the three parts, the folders, the rules about who may use whom, and how the team writes down its decisions. The later chapters zoom into each part.

What the project is, and why

This project is an ad-free sudoku game for the iPhone. You can play a generated puzzle. You can also point the camera at a sudoku in a newspaper or a puzzle book. The phone reads the grid and loads it into the game. Then you can play it, solve it or ask for a hint.

The project has two reasons to exist:

  1. Learning and portfolio. The developer builds the whole chain once and does it properly: data, training, export, on-device inference, a live camera pipeline, a React Native app and a small cloud backend. All of it is documented.
  2. Replace an ad-heavy app. The developer plays sudoku every day. The old app shows too many ads. This app has none, works without a network and starts at once.
What is not a goal A release in the App Store is possible, but it is not a goal. No decision may block it, and no decision must aim for it. Source: chapter 1 of the architecture docs.

The three parts

The architecture docs say the system has three parts. Each part solves one problem. The figure shows how they connect.

Part 1

Game

Four levels: easy, medium, hard and expert. Notes, undo, a timer, pause and mistake highlighting. Hints that teach the technique. Stats and streaks. It works from the first day, even without any machine learning.

Part 2

Scan

A live outline snaps onto the grid. A small neural network reads each cell: the digit and its kind (printed, handwritten or notes). You check the result on a correction screen before you play.

Part 3

Backend

An account with Clerk. A copy of your finished games on AWS, so a second phone shows the same history. A private bucket for scans that you choose to share. One Lambda and one table keep the cost low.

Analogy Think of a pocket calculator with an optional cloud notebook. The calculator (game and scan) always works. The notebook (backend) only saves a copy of your finished sums, so you can read them on another device.

Quality goals, in order

When two goals fight, the higher one wins. The list is in chapter 1.2 of the architecture docs. It explains many choices that look odd later.

#GoalWhy it matters
1Recognition correctnessOne wrong digit breaks the puzzle. The whole grid must be right, not the average cell. Chapter 5 shows how the project measures this as grid-exact accuracy.
2Smooth scanningThe live outline is the feature people notice. Jitter makes the app feel broken.
3Offline and privateA daily game that needs a network is worse than the app it replaces. Camera frames stay on the phone.
4Understandable architectureIt is a learning and portfolio project. A reader must follow every boundary. This guide is part of that goal.
5Everyday gameInstant start, no lost progress and a hint when you are stuck.

The scanner reads a digit wrongly in 1 cell out of 81. Which quality goal does this hurt most?

Goal 1. A sudoku with one wrong given digit has no valid solution, or a different one. That is why the team scores the model on whole grids and not on single cells.

Player

Plays generated puzzles, scans paper puzzles and corrects the digits that the model got wrong. The player touches the screen and aims the camera. Nothing else.

Paper sudoku

A page in a newspaper or a book. The camera sees it. The photo, the flattened grid and the cell images stay on the phone (SUD-02). The only exception is the setting Help improve the app, which sends a scan photo for training. The player can turn it off. A photo from the photo library never leaves.

ML workstation

The developer's Apple Silicon Mac. It trains the digit model with PyTorch on the GPU (MPS). The model reaches the app as a file. It travels through the repository, not through the network, and only after a gate (SUD-06). Chapter 5 tells this story.

GitHub

Holds the repository and runs CI. CI runs the tests and the boundary check on Linux, builds the iOS app for the simulator on a macOS runner, and deploys the backend with an AWS role (OIDC, no stored keys). Workflows are in .github/workflows/.

Sudoku app

The centre of the system. One Expo app with the game, the scanner and local storage in SQLite. It needs no server. The folder is mobile/. The next chapters open it.

TestFlight and the App Store

Apple distributes builds. TestFlight is how the developer installs the daily-use build. A public App Store release is possible, not planned.

Clerk

A hosted sign-in service. It holds the accounts, sends the verification emails and signs the tokens. The app uses @clerk/expo. The backend only checks a token. It never sees a password (ADR-027).

AWS backend

One personal account in eu-west-1. An HTTP API, a Lambda for the sync, one DynamoDB table, a second Lambda for the scan upload and a private S3 bucket. SST builds it. A budget and a kill switch keep the bill below 10 euro a month (SUD-11). Chapter 6 opens it.

Vercel web site

A static home page and the privacy page at sudoku.felixvandemaele.be. It has no JavaScript, no cookies and no analytics. It has no arrow to the app: nothing in mobile/ or shared/ imports web/.

Why so few arrows into the cloud? Only two arrows leave the phone for a server the developer runs: the sync (after sign-in) and the optional scan upload. Everything else on the left side of the figure is development tooling. This small surface is what makes SUD-01 and SUD-02 easy to keep.

The monorepo, folder by folder

The problem: the project needs two programming languages. The app and the backend use TypeScript. The model training uses Python. They change together: a new model format touches the training code, the export and the app loader in one go. Two repositories would split that change in two.

The answer (ADR-001): one repository with two toolchains side by side. pnpm workspaces run the TypeScript. uv runs the Python. One justfile at the root lists every command for both.

ADR-029 then gave the root a clear shape: one folder per area. Click a folder to see what it holds, which stack it uses and who may import it.

mobile/ — the Expo app

The iOS app: game UI, scan UI, the scanner module, storage, account and sync. It uses React Native 0.86 and Expo SDK 57, with Expo Router for the screens. The source is in mobile/src/, one folder per feature: home, game, settings, stats, techniques and scan, plus scanner, storage, account, sync and scanShare. Code that two features use lives in components/, hooks/, preferences/, utils/ and constants/. Chapter 3 opens this folder.

shared/core — @sudoku/core

The sudoku brain: board, rules, solver, technique solver, generator, hints, streak and clock format. It is pure TypeScript. It imports no React Native, no Expo and no I/O (SUD-04). That is why its tests run in milliseconds in plain Node with Vitest. The app imports its source directly, with no build step. See chapter 2.

shared/sync-contract

The zod schemas for the sync API. The app and the Lambda both import this one package, so a change in the wire format fails in both places at compile time. It may depend on zod only. See chapter 6.

shared/scan-contract

The same idea for the scan upload (ADR-030): the scan record schema, the size limit of 1,000,000 bytes and the header names. The app builds a record. The scans Lambda validates it. Note that the root boundaries script does not cruise this folder yet. It checks shared/core/src and shared/sync-contract/src only.

backend/api — the Lambdas

Two Lambda handlers. The first, src/handler.ts, serves POST /v1/sync and the account delete. It maps a Clerk token to our own user ID and reads and writes DynamoDB. The second, src/scans/handler.ts, hands out a signed upload URL for the scan bucket. It never imports the app and never imports backend/infra.

backend/kill-switch

A small Lambda that exists once, in production. When the AWS budget reaches 9 dollars, it sets an SSM flag per stage, drops the route throttle to 0 and the concurrency to 0. This protects SUD-11, the spending cap. just api-on <stage> clears it.

backend/infra — SST

Infrastructure as code with SST 4: one file per area (storage.ts, api.ts, kill-switch.ts, budget.ts, and more). It may read exactly one Lambda file: backend/api/src/tableDesign.ts, so the test table matches the real table. sst.config.ts stays at the repository root because SST expects it there.

web/ — the static site

A home page and a privacy page. A small build script writes web/dist/. The privacy page is built from docs/privacy/privacy-policy.md, the only source. Vercel hosts it. No other folder imports it.

ml/ — the Python project

A stand-alone uv project. It holds the dataset (ml/dataset/), the labelling tools, training, evaluation, export and the promote step. The code is in ml/src/sudoku_ml/. It uses PyTorch on Apple MPS and exports TFLite. It never imports the TypeScript side (SUD-07). Chapter 5 opens it.

shared/model-contract

The agreement between Python and the app about the model: input size, outputs, labels and the manifest. The spec itself is the Markdown file model-contract.md. These are plain files. This folder is not a package, and it is not in the pnpm workspace.

shared/fixtures

Test data that Python and TypeScript both read: public-safe sudoku photos with their true grids (scans/) and reference cell images for preprocessing parity (preprocess/). The model never trains on a fixture cell (SUD-10). Private fixtures stay out of git (SUD-08).

tools/

Developer scripts: the custom oxlint plugin, the icon renderer, the layout check for SUD-12, the link check and the scan delete tool. Also tools/label-app/, a Vite and React app that the developer uses to label training cells. It talks only to a Python server on 127.0.0.1 (ADR-034).

docs/

The architecture docs (Arc42), the operations runbook, the privacy policy, research notes and this guide. A later section of this chapter explains how they fit together.

The root files

justfile (every command), package.json and pnpm-workspace.yaml (the TypeScript workspace), sst.config.ts (the SST app), .dependency-cruiser.cjs (the boundary rules), AGENTS.md and LICENSE (MIT). SUD-12 says the root holds only the entries that ADR-029 lists. just lint checks it with tools/layout/check-layout.mjs.

What counts as a package?

A folder is a pnpm package only if pnpm-workspace.yaml lists it. Today that list holds eight entries: mobile, web, backend/api, backend/kill-switch, shared/core, shared/sync-contract, shared/scan-contract and tools/label-app. The Python project, the model contract and the fixtures are not in the list. Python cannot use a pnpm package, so a fake package would only mislead.

Why not Turborepo or Nx? ADR-001 and ADR-029 looked at both. The repository has few packages. Python would stay outside the task graph anyway. A task cache does not pay yet. The reassessment trigger is written down: if CI takes more than 15 minutes, add a cache.

You want to add a folder experiments/ at the repository root. What happens?

SUD-12 lets the root hold only the folders that ADR-029 lists. tools/layout/check-layout.mjs runs in just lint. A new top-level folder needs a new ADR.

Two languages, one repository

The most important sentence in the repository is SUD-07: the Python side and the TypeScript side share files only. Neither imports the other. The figure shows what that means.

Analogy Two kitchens, one in Italy and one in Japan, cook from the same recipe cards. The cards say exactly what goes in and what comes out. The kitchens never share a stove, a knife or a cook. If one kitchen changes, the cards tell the other what to expect.

That is what the model contract is. It is a written agreement: the model takes a 32 × 32 image and returns a digit and a kind. Both Python and TypeScript have a test that checks the agreement against the same reference files (shared/fixtures/preprocess/). If one side drifts, the test fails. Chapter 5 goes deeper.

Pitfall: the model is not in git The architecture docs say the promoted model lives in mobile/assets/models/. The file cell-classifier.tflite is git-ignored, because it learned from photos that may not be published (SUD-08, ADR-007). A fresh clone plays every game. The scanner needs a model: train one with just train and install it with just install-model. Where the docs and the code disagree, follow the code and the README.

Who may import whom

The problem: folders do not stop a developer from writing import ... from '../../scanner/...' from the wrong place. A boundary that only lives in a README rots in weeks. This project turns its boundaries into failing checks.

The tool: dependency-cruiser. It reads every import in the TypeScript code and compares the arrow with the rules in .dependency-cruiser.cjs. just boundaries runs it. just lint includes it, and CI runs it on every pull request that touches TypeScript.

Analogy A restaurant. The dining room never walks into the kitchen (SUD-03). The kitchen has one pass-through window (SUD-05). The accounts office cannot open the till (SUD-04, a pure core). Rules like these only work if a manager checks them every day. dependency-cruiser is that manager.

How dependency-cruiser runs here

The root package.json has the script boundaries. It runs depcruise over mobile/index.ts, mobile/src, shared/core/src, shared/sync-contract/src, backend/api/src, backend/kill-switch/src, backend/infra and sst.config.ts, with the config .dependency-cruiser.cjs.

  • Static analysis. The tool parses the source. It does not run it. It sees each import as an arrow between two files and checks the arrow against every rule.
  • TypeScript aware. The option tsPreCompilationDeps counts imports that only exist for types. The file tsconfig.boundaries.json copies the app aliases (@/, @assets/), because the Expo base config cannot be read from the root.
  • The model file is special. The rule no-unresolvable ignores .tflite, since the file is git-ignored and CI cannot resolve it.
  • The allowlist is a debt list. The constant CROSS_FEATURE_ALLOWLIST at the top of the config names the 11 imports between features that still exist. Each line has a comment that says who imports it. The rule: remove a line when the code moves to a shared folder, and delete the list when it is empty.
  • Severity is error everywhere. There are no warnings to ignore.

mobile/src/scan/ needs a helper from mobile/src/game/utils/gameSession.utils.ts. What is the right fix?

ADR-029 (amendment 2026-10-23): code that two features use belongs in a shared folder. The allowlist is for debt that already exists. A new line makes the debt bigger.

The binding constraints

The problem: a project of this size has a few rules that must never break, and a few hundred small choices that may change. If every choice looks equally important, the rules get lost.

The answer: the file 00-DECISIONS.md separates the two. Constraints (SUD-xx) bind: a change that breaks one makes the solution unsound. Decisions (ADR-xxx) inform: they hold until someone re-decides them. The file has a hard cap of 20 constraints. It lists 12 today, ordered by impact: Systemic, Cross-concept and Concept.

The rule for every agent and developer Obey every SUD-xx constraint. If a change would break one, stop and say so. Do not work around it. Do not change an accepted decision in code alone: write or amend an ADR first (AGENTS.md, rules 1 and 2).

The three systemic constraints

SUD-01

Offline first

The game and the scanner work fully without a network. Analogy: a pocket calculator. It does not call a server to add two numbers. How the code keeps it: the model runs on the phone (ADR-003), puzzles generate on the phone, storage is SQLite, and the sync is optional and silent when it fails.

SUD-02

Privacy

Camera frames and cell images never leave the device. A scan photo leaves only through the setting Help improve the app, on by default and easy to turn off. A library image never leaves. Analogy: a photo booth that keeps your pictures unless you press the share button. In code: mobile/src/scanShare/ is the only module that uploads a scan.

SUD-07

Files only between Python and TypeScript

ml/ and the TypeScript side share shared/model-contract/, shared/fixtures/ and the promoted model, and nothing else. Analogy: two kitchens with the same recipe cards. See the figure in the section above.

How the team writes down decisions

The problem: one developer, side-project hours and a project that runs for months. After a break, nobody remembers why the model has two heads or why the root has seven folders. Chat history is lost. Source code shows what, but never why not the other way.

The answer: the project documents its architecture with Arc42. The docs live in docs/architecture/. Click a box to see what each part is for.

AGENTS.md

The entry point. It lists the rules that bind all work, the writing style, the comment rules, the work tracking and the git rules. It has a table that says "if you need X, read file Y". Claude Code reads it through CLAUDE.md, which only imports it. Other agents read it directly. See AGENTS.md.

The Arc42 chapters

Numbered files 01- to 12-. Examples: 01 goals, 03 system context, 05 building blocks, 07 deployment, 09 the list of ADRs, 10 quality requirements, 12 the glossary. Each file starts with front matter: status: DRAFT or status: ACCEPTED. They describe the system as a whole, from several views.

00-DECISIONS.md

An index, not a place for reasoning. It has a table of constraints (SUD-xx) and a table of decisions (ADR-xxx), with impact, date, owner and a link to the source. A bold Pending note marks an ADR with the status Proposed. Open this file first when you wonder "is there a rule about this?". Read it here.

Concepts

One file per idea that spans many parts: scan-pipeline.md, game-screen.md, history-sync.md, model-contract.md, hints.md, scan-collection.md and splash-screen.md. Each has the sections Why, What, a diagram, Open questions and Constraints. The chapters of this guide build on them.

ADRs

An ADR holds the reasoning for one decision. The sections are fixed: Context, Alternatives Evaluated, Decision, Consequences and Reassessment Triggers, plus a metadata table at the top. There are 35 ADRs now. See the lifecycle below.

The glossary

The dictionary of the project's writing style. "One word, one meaning" is the first rule. If a document needs a new term, it first goes in 12-glossary.md. The tooltips in this guide use the same definitions.

The life of an ADR

An ADR is not written once and forgotten. It moves through states. The stepper shows a typical path. Click Next.

1. A need appears

Every change starts from a GitHub issue. If the issue asks for a choice that is hard to undo, the developer proposes an ADR. Example: "where should the backend folders go?" became ADR-029.

2. Write it as Proposed

The ADR has a metadata table (project, status, date, contact) and five sections: Context, Alternatives Evaluated, Decision, Consequences and Reassessment Triggers. The status is Proposed. A reader can see the rejected options, and that is the main point.

3. Update the index in the same change

The rule in AGENTS.md: update 00-DECISIONS.md and 09-architecture-decisions.md in the same change. A decision that is not in the index does not exist for the next reader. Every relative link must resolve before the commit.

4. Build it, and let the code test the idea

The code is written. Some ADRs wait for a real-world check. Example: ADR-002 (React Native and the scanner engine) still says Pending the walking skeleton. ADR-030 waits for an iPhone check. The pending note names exactly what it waits for.

5. Accept it with a dated amendment

When the code proves the decision, the status moves from Proposed to Accepted, with an amendment such as "Amendment 2026-10-22: accepted". The decision itself does not change. Example: ADR-008 got this amendment after the boundary rules ran in CI.

6. Change it with an amendment, or replace it

A small change becomes a new, dated amendment at the end of the same ADR. ADR-011 (puzzle generation) has many. A big change gets a new ADR that says "Supersedes ADR-NNN". Nobody rewrites the old decision, so the history stays honest. ADR-029 superseded the folder layout of ADR-001, ADR-008 and ADR-018. Their other decisions stand.

Why the "Reassessment Triggers" section? A decision is right for a time. The trigger says when to look again. ADR-029 names three: a second deployable app, CI above 15 minutes, and SST moving its config file. This turns "we should revisit this someday" into a concrete test.

The journey of one feature

You now know the parts, the folders and the rules. Follow one puzzle from paper to the cloud. This is the thread that the next chapters pull. Click Next, and use the links to jump to a chapter.

1. Scan: the camera finds the grid

Every camera frame goes to a VisionCamera frame processor. It downscales the frame, finds the largest four-corner shape and reports it. The scan UI smooths the corners and draws the outline in Skia. When the same grid stays in view long enough, the scan locks on and takes a full photo. Files: mobile/src/scanner/useGridDetector.ts, findGrid.ts, mobile/src/scan/utils/lockOn.utils.ts. The scan UI uses the scanner only through src/scanner/index.ts (SUD-05). More in chapter 4.

2. Recognise: 81 cells become digits

On capture, the scanner flattens the page and finds the 100 grid intersections. It cuts out each cell from its own four corners, which fixes a curved book page piece by piece. The model reads all 81 cell images in one batch and returns a digit and a kind for each: none, printed, handwritten or notes. File: mobile/src/scanner/recognise.ts. The model file comes from ml/ (chapter 5).

3. Correct: the player checks the result

A correction screen shows the grid. The player fixes a wrong digit or switches a cell between given (printed) and mine (handwritten). A half-solved book puzzle loads with the player's own digits as the player's. If sharing is on, the corrected scan can go to the training set later, through mobile/src/scanShare/. Files: mobile/src/scan/correctionStore.ts, the hooks in mobile/src/scan/hooks/useCorrectionSession/.

4. Play: the same game, from a scan or from a seed

A generated puzzle and a scanned puzzle enter the game through the same function, createPuzzleStart (mobile/src/game/utils/puzzleStart.utils.ts). The rules, the solver and the hints come from @sudoku/core. A game session (gameSession.utils.ts) holds the reducer, the clock, the pause and the saves. The solver that grades a puzzle is the same solver that builds its hints. See chapter 2 and chapter 3.

5. Finish: the result is saved

When the grid is complete, or the player loses or gives up, the game writes a history entry through the storage repositories (mobile/src/storage/HistoryRepository.ts). The stats count it in SQL (StatsRepository.ts). The rule that decides whether a time counts as a best time is countsForBestTime in @sudoku/core. Everything here works with no network.

6. Sync: finished games reach the cloud

Only a signed-in player syncs, and only finished games. Storage tells the sync that something changed, through a subscription, because storage may not import the sync. SyncService (mobile/src/sync/SyncService.ts) sends the waiting list in one POST /v1/sync and pulls changes since a server cursor. The Lambda checks the Clerk token and writes to DynamoDB with push.ts and pull.ts. A failure is silent: the game never waits for the network. See chapter 6.

Analogy A newspaper puzzle goes on a trip. A photographer takes the picture (scan). An assistant reads it and writes the numbers down (recognise). You check the assistant's work (correct). You solve it (play). You file the result in your own notebook (finish). A copy of the notebook page goes to a safe in another city (sync). The assistant and the notebook work with no phone line. Only the safe needs one.

Why does the game storage tell the sync about a change through a subscription, and not by importing the sync?

The sync (mobile/src/sync/) runs in the app, and it imports storage. If storage imported the sync, you would get a cycle, and the game would depend on the account. The subscription reverses the direction of knowledge without reversing the import.
