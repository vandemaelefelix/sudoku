Chapter 4

Scanning a paper sudoku

Point the phone at a paper sudoku and, about a second later, the puzzle is ready to play. This chapter follows that second from the camera sensor to the board, one step at a time.

Before you read This chapter uses the words frame, thread and worklet. Each one is explained when it first appears. You need no computer vision background. If you want to know how the digit model is trained, read chapter 5 after this one.

The whole journey in one picture

A scan has one hard job. The phone sees a flat, tilted, badly lit sheet of paper. The game needs 81 numbers. Between the two sits a chain of small steps. Each step does one simple thing.

Analogy: a photographer and a lab Think of a street photographer. While the person walks, the photographer watches through the viewfinder and quietly follows the subject. That is cheap work, and it happens all the time. When the shot is right, the photographer presses the button once. Then the film goes to a lab. The lab does the slow, careful work, but only one time. The scan pipeline has the same shape: a cheap live stage, then an expensive capture stage.

Click through the stepper below. It shows the full path. The colour of a box tells you which thread does the work. Chapter 3 explained why the app needs more than one thread. The next section explains it again, for the scanner.

1. A frame arrives

The camera sensor makes a new picture about 30 times a second. Each picture is a frame. VisionCamera hands every frame to a small function called the frame processor. It runs on the camera thread, not on the thread that draws the screen.

2. Resize to 360 × 640

A full frame has millions of pixels. Finding a big square does not need that many. useGridDetector (mobile/src/scanner/useGridDetector.ts) shrinks the frame on the GPU to 360 × 640 pixels, RGB. This is the only size the live stage sees.

3. Find every plausible grid

findGrids (mobile/src/scanner/findGrid.ts) turns the picture grey, blurs it, finds the ink and traces outlines. It keeps every outline that has four corners. The next section shows each sub-step.

4. Choose one grid

A page can hold several sudokus. chooseGrid picks the one the player aims at: the grid nearest the middle of the view. It sticks to that grid until another one is clearly nearer.

5. Smooth the corners

The raw corners jitter by a pixel or two from frame to frame. smoothQuad (mobile/src/scan/utils/tracking.utils.ts) uses a filter that holds the outline steady when the phone is still and follows quickly when it moves. This runs on the UI thread.

6. Lock on

The lock-on tracker watches the grid. If the same grid stays in view for 1 second and the phone does not clearly move, it fires once. The player can also press the shutter button at any time.

7. Take the photo, find the grid again

VisionCamera takes one full-resolution photo. recognise() shrinks a copy to 640 px and runs the grid search again, near the locked grid. The corners always come from the photo, because the hand moved a little since lock-on.

8. Flatten the grid

The four corners define a skewed shape. warpGrid (mobile/src/scanner/warp.ts) stretches it into a perfect 576 × 576 square. That square is 9 × 9 cells of 64 × 64 pixels.

9. Cut 81 cells and prepare them

sliceCells cuts the square into 81 cell images. preprocessCells turns each into 32 × 32 grey numbers, with ink near 1.0 and paper near 0.0. One array, 81 × 32 × 32, goes to the model.

10. Ask the model

One TFLite call reads all 81 cells. It answers two questions per cell: which digit, and what kind of mark (printed, handwritten, notes, or nothing). The answer also carries a confidence.

11. The player checks

The correction screen shows what the model read. Cells with low confidence get a ring. The player fixes a digit, or switches a cell between "given" and "mine".

12. The play gate

Before "Play this puzzle" turns on, @sudoku/core checks that the printed digits have exactly one solution. Then the game loads. See chapter 2 for the solver.

Why two stages (ADR-004)

Why not do everything on every frame? Because the work is very different in cost and in purpose. The decision is in ADR-004.

A frame arrives every 33 ms. If the live work took longer than that, frames would pile up and the outline would lag behind the paper. So the live stage stays tiny: it only finds corners. All the heavy work waits for the one moment that matters.

Why this way? Fixing a bad still photo afterwards needs a lot of code. A live camera lets the player fix most problems: hold the phone better, tilt it less, move into the light. The outline tells the player when the view is good. So the pipeline can stay simple and still work well.
On device only (ADR-003) Both stages run on the phone. No frame, no photo and no cell image goes to a server for recognition. ADR-003 says why: it works offline, the live outline needs zero delay, and it costs nothing per scan. The model is smaller than 1 MB, so it fits easily.
Where the docs and the code differ ADR-004 and the scan-pipeline concept describe a more ambitious capture stage: find all 100 grid crossings, then flatten each cell from its own four corners. That would correct a page that curves near a book spine. The code does not do this yet. recognise.ts flattens the whole grid once from the four corners, then cuts it into equal 64 × 64 squares. This chapter follows the code. Where the plan is bigger, the text says so.

The camera and the threads

The problem: a phone does many things at once

React Native runs your app logic on one thread, the JS thread. It handles taps, state and React renders. If that thread is busy for 100 ms, taps feel stuck and animations freeze. A scan needs heavy maths on 30 frames every second. If that maths ran on the JS thread, the whole app would stutter.

Analogy: a restaurant kitchen One cook cannot chop, grill, plate and serve at the same time. A real kitchen has stations. The grill cook never waits for the dishwasher. The scanner works the same way. Each job gets its own station (thread), and the stations pass small notes to each other instead of carrying whole trays.

Camera thread: the frame processor

VisionCamera calls the onFrame worklet for each frame, on its own thread. useFrameTracking (mobile/src/scan/hooks/useFrameTracking/useFrameTracking.ts) calls the detector from useGridDetector. The detector resizes the frame, runs findGrids, then runs chooseGrid.

The state of chooseGrid (which grid is tracked) stays on this thread, in a createSynchronizable box. So no corner goes through the JS thread. The worklet then writes the answer into a shared value called detection.

UI thread: smoothing, scene, drawing

The UI thread draws the screen, 60 times a second. A useAnimatedReaction watches detection. When it changes, smoothQuad smooths the corners. Then advance (mobile/src/scan/effects/engine.ts) steps the scene and the lock-on tracker, and the active effect draws with Skia.

Everything here is a worklet, so it needs no trip to the JS thread. The budget is 4 ms per frame for the scene and one effect (ADR-014).

JS thread: the story of the scan

The JS thread holds the slow-moving state: the capture session (useCaptureSession), the correction session, and the React screens. It only hears about the live stage once, when the lock-on fires. It then asks the scanner to do the heavy work and waits for the answer.

Because it does not do the heavy work itself, the JS thread stays free for taps.

Scanner runtime: the one-shot heavy work

createScanner (mobile/src/scanner/scanner.ts) makes one extra worklet runtime named scanner with createWorkletRuntime. recognise() runs there. It reads the photo, finds the grid again, flattens it, cuts 81 cells and runs the model.

A flatten alone takes over 100 ms on a phone. On the scanner runtime, the transition animation still runs at full speed.

What is a worklet?

A worklet is a normal JavaScript function with the text 'worklet' as its first line. A build step copies it so it can run on any thread, not only the JS thread. The price: a worklet cannot reach most of the outside world. It can read plain data and call other worklets. That is why the scanner files use plain arithmetic and plain objects everywhere, such as rowOf(cell) in mobile/src/utils/grid.utils.ts instead of a lookup table. 

// abridged from useFrameTracking.ts
const frameOutput = useFrameOutput({
  pixelFormat: 'yuv',
  onFrame(frame) {
    'worklet'                        // runs on the camera thread
    try {
      const grid = detectGrid(frame)  // resize + findGrids + chooseGrid
      detection.set({ quad: grid?.quad ?? null, timestamp: frame.timestamp, ... })
    } finally {
      frame.dispose()                 // give the camera buffer back
    }
  },
})

Two details matter. First, the frame buffer belongs to the camera, so the code calls frame.dispose() in a finally block, also after an error. Second, a shared value is a small box of memory that the camera thread writes and the UI thread reads. It is how four corners cross from one thread to the other without the JS thread.

Pitfall: garbage collection is late in worklets OpenCV objects (Mat, PointVector) live in native memory. In a worklet runtime, the JavaScript garbage collector can run late, so native memory would grow. Every function in scanner/ that makes an OpenCV object keeps it in a list called owned and calls release() in a finally block. The walking-skeleton check in ADR-002 was "no memory growth over 5 minutes of scanning".

Live grid detection, step by step

The problem. In a 360 × 640 picture, find the four corners of the sudoku. The picture can be dark on one side, grainy, tilted, and full of other things: text, a mug, last week's solution.

The idea. A sudoku is the biggest connected network of dark lines on a page. Find the dark pixels. Group them into connected blobs. Take a big blob. If the outline of the blob is a four-sided shape, it is probably the grid.

1. The frame

The frame is a portrait picture, 360 × 640. The page is lit unevenly: the right side is in shadow. Around the sudoku there is clutter, which the later steps must ignore.

2. Grey and blur

Colour does not help to find a dark line, so cvtColor makes the picture grey. GaussianBlur with a 5 × 5 window then smooths away sensor grain. The thin grey line in the chart is one noisy row. The thick line is the same row after the blur.

3. Adaptive threshold

Now each pixel becomes ink (white) or not ink (black). The test is relative: a pixel is ink if it is darker than the average of its 11 × 11 neighbourhood, minus 2. A shadow changes the neighbourhood too, so the test still works in shadow. The lab below shows why that matters.

4. Contours

findContours walks around each blob of white pixels. With RETR_EXTERNAL it reports only outer outlines, not the holes inside. The grid lines form one connected network, so the outline of the whole network is one big shape. findGrids drops outlines under 10 % of the frame area and sorts the rest, largest first.

5. Polygon approximation

The raw outline has hundreds of points that wobble by a pixel. approxPolyDP with a tolerance of 2 % of the outline length keeps only the corners. A grid gives exactly 4. Anything else is not a quad, and the code skips it.

6. Order and check

OpenCV gives the four points in any order. toGridDetection (mobile/src/scanner/quad.ts) orders them, scales them to 0..1, and drops a shape that is not convex, too small, or cut by the frame edge. What is left is a GridDetection.

Three useful consequences No ML in the live stage. It is classic computer vision, so it is fast and predictable. It finds any big four-sided blob of lines, not only a sudoku. Later rules (area, border, aiming) pick the right one. The unit is the "quad": four points, always in the order TL, TR, BR, BL. Every later function relies on that order.

Deep dive: how the corners get their order

OpenCV returns the four polygon points in the order it walked around the outline. That order depends on where the walk started. The rest of the app needs a fixed order. orderCorners in mobile/src/scanner/quad.ts does it in three moves:

  1. Find the centre: the average of the four points.
  2. Sort the points by the angle atan2(y - cy, x - cx) from the centre. Because the screen's y axis points down, a rising angle runs clockwise on screen. The ring is now in order, but it may start anywhere.
  3. Rotate the ring so it starts at the point with the smallest x + y. That is the corner nearest the top-left of the screen.

The result is TL, TR, BR, BL, clockwise. A page photographed upside down still gives TL at the top-left of the screen. That is fine: the model reads digits, and the transition only needs consistency. Rotation on the sensor is handled elsewhere. The resizer version used here turns a sideways frame the wrong way up, so useGridDetector calls turnHalf to rotate the four corners by 180 degrees. That is cheaper than turning 230,400 pixels.

A small detail: the code adds 0.5 when it normalises, (x + 0.5) / width. OpenCV puts the centre of a pixel on a whole number. A normalised coordinate treats a pixel as a square from 0 to 1. The half-pixel shift keeps the two views consistent. The inverse, toPhotoPixels, subtracts 0.5 again.

Choosing one grid: the aiming rule

The problem. findGrids returns every plausible quad. A puzzle book page can hold two sudokus of the same size. If the app picked by "largest", the outline would flip between them as the phone shakes.

The idea. Let the player aim. The grid nearest the middle of the view wins. Once a grid is tracked, it stays chosen, until another grid is clearly nearer the middle for a short time. This is chooseGrid in mobile/src/scanner/chooseGrid.ts. It is a pure function: it takes the old state and the new detections and returns the new state. The camera thread keeps the state between frames.

RuleValueWhat it prevents
Size filter MIN_AREA_SHARE0.6A grid under 60 % of the biggest grid never counts. Last week's small solution does not steal the aim
Tracking tolerance TRACK_TOLERANCE0.5 of a grid sideDecides "is this the same grid as in the last frame". A corner may move up to half a side

Switch dwell SWITCH_DWELL0.3 sA short swing of the phone does not switch the grid
Loss hold LOSS_HOLD0.25 sOne missed detection does not forget the grid. During the hold, no outline shows, so it does not jump to a neighbour

A switch is reported as a count, not as a flag. The UI thread can skip a frame under load. A flag could be missed. A count that went up cannot be missed. When the count changes, useFrameTracking starts the smoothing again, so the outline jumps in one frame to the new grid.

A still image and a shutter press with no grid in view have no aim. They use only the "first find" rule, firstFind: the big-enough grid nearest the centre.

The live stage uses an adaptive threshold and not one fixed brightness cut. Why?

The test is "darker than the local average". A shadow lowers the paper and the lines together, so the lines still stand out. A fixed cut would turn the whole shadow black or lose the lines in the lit area.

Smoothing: a steady outline

The problem. Even on a perfectly still phone, the four detected corners move by a pixel or two from frame to frame. The camera has noise, and the threshold edge flips by a pixel. Drawn raw, the outline would shiver. A shivering outline looks broken. It also feeds false motion into the lock-on rule.

The obvious fix, and its flaw. Average the last few frames. This calms the shiver, but it lags. When the player moves the phone, the outline trails behind the paper. You can have steady or responsive, not both, with a fixed average.

Analogy: car suspension that adapts A soft spring feels great on a smooth road but wobbles in a corner. A stiff spring is crisp in the corner but rattles on the road. The One Euro filter is a spring that changes its stiffness. When the corner is still, it is very soft and removes the shiver. When the corner moves fast, it becomes stiff and follows with almost no lag. The paper is Casiez et al., CHI 2012.

smoothQuad in mobile/src/scan/utils/tracking.utils.ts applies the filter to each of the 8 numbers (4 corners, x and y). In one sentence: move a fraction alpha of the way from the old value to the new one, and let alpha grow with the speed. 

speed      = smoothed estimate of |new - old| / dt           (cutoff DERIVATIVE_CUTOFF = 1 Hz)
cutoff     = MIN_CUTOFF + BETA * speed                      (0.5 Hz + 120 * speed)
tau        = 1 / (2 * pi * cutoff)
alpha      = 1 / (1 + tau / dt)                              (0..1)
new_value  = old + alpha * (target - old)

Two other rules live in the same file. If no corner arrives, the old outline stays for HOLD = 0.25 s, then it goes. A long gap or a switch of grid starts the filter new, so the outline jumps once instead of sliding across the page.

Lock-on: knowing when to shoot

The problem. The app takes the photo by itself. It must pick the right moment: the grid is in view, big enough, and the player means it. A photo taken during a swing is blurred. A photo taken when the player only passes over a page is unwanted.

The first idea and why it failed (ADR-019). "Shoot when the grid is steady." The first version measured speed between two detections. The developer measured a real hand-held iPhone over a still page. The median speed was 28.8 pt/s and the 90th percentile 97 pt/s. The steady limit was about 15 pt/s. The phone counted as steady in only 16 % of the frames, so the scan never locked on. Nobody holds a phone perfectly still.

Why this way? From speed to drift ADR-019 replaced "speed between two frames" with the drift: the largest net corner move over the last DRIFT_WINDOW = 0.3 s, divided by the time. Hand tremor goes back and forth, so over 0.3 s it cancels out. A real pan goes one way and stays. One number now separates the two cases for every hand.

The lock-on tracker is stepLockOn in mobile/src/scan/utils/lockOn.utils.ts. It runs on the UI thread, once per frame, and it fires once. The scan locks on when all of this is true:

ConditionConstantMeaning
The scene is warmWARMUP = 1.0 sWait one second after the camera screen opens, so the camera can set exposure and focus. A Retake starts a new scene, so the warm-up runs again
The grid is big enoughMIN_GRID_WIDTH = 0.35The mean of the top and bottom edges is at least 35 % of the view width. Once big, it stays big down to 32 % (GRID_WIDTH_HYSTERESIS = 0.03), so a grid near the limit does not flicker

The line that holds the whole rule is short. The dwell counts from the latest of four moments: 

const since = Math.max(lock.foundAt, lock.movedAt, lock.startedAt + WARMUP, lock.bigSince)
const ready = !moving && lock.bigSince >= 0
lock.progress = ready ? clamp((t - since) / LOCK_DWELL, 0, 1) : 0

Every bad event pushes one of these four moments forward, and so it pushes the lock-on later. The player sees the progress as the gathered dots settling and brightening. Try it yourself:

Three ways to start a scan, one winner

The player can also press the shutter button at any time, or import a photo. A shutter press and the automatic lock-on can fire in the same instant. A tiny object, the lock latch (claimLock in the same file), lives on the JS thread. The first caller gets true and starts the scan. Every later caller gets false. There are three sources: 'auto', 'shutter' and 'import'. A scan can never start twice.

Capture: from the locked quad to the photo

At lock-on, VisionCamera takes one full-resolution photo with capturePhoto(). The photo has far more pixels than the 360 × 640 frame, and it is sharper. Now the app has a puzzle: the locked quad is in frame coordinates. The photo is a different picture. How do the corners map across?

Normalised coordinates

The live stage reports every corner as two numbers from 0 to 1: x = 0.5 means "half way across", whatever the pixel width is. This survives different resolutions, aspect ratios and orientations. The same quad works on the 360 × 640 frame and on a 3000-pixel-wide photo, as long as both show the same view. A 16:9 video frame and a 16:9 photo have the same field of view. sameAspect checks it with a tolerance of 1 %. If the aspects differ, the code drops the locked quad and searches the whole photo (runCapture in mobile/src/scan/hooks/useCaptureSession/captureRun.ts).

The hand moved: re-detect

Between lock-on and the shutter, the phone moves a little, so the locked quad no longer fits the photo exactly. A one-percent error on a 3000 px photo is 30 pixels, enough to cut a digit in half. So recognise() does not reuse the locked corners. It runs the grid search again on the photo. The locked quad only answers the question "which grid?".

1. Start with the locked quad

recognise(photo, quad, ...) gets the quad as normalised numbers. It first asks the photo for an upright image with photo.toImage(), and makes a downscaled copy with a long side of 640 px, the same size as the live stage.

2. Search near it

locateGrid (mobile/src/scanner/recognise.ts) crops the small copy to the grown bounding box and runs findGrids on the crop. fromCrop maps the found quads back to whole-photo coordinates.

3. Take the nearest match

nearest picks the candidate with the smallest cornerOffset that is at most 0.5. The corners that come out are the ones from the photo, and they go on to the flatten step. The result remembers corners: 'redetected' and the offset, so a developer can see how far the hand moved.

A shutter press with no grid in view has no locked quad. Then locateGrid uses firstFind on the whole photo and reports corners: 'found'.

The photo is rotated in the file A camera sensor stores pixels in its own orientation, and an EXIF tag says how to turn them. photo.toImage() applies the tag. But toRawPixelData() on that image still gives the sensor buffer. The trick in recogniseImage is to call image.resize(...) first. A resize draws the pixels upright, so the raw pixels of the resized image are upright. Both the small search copy and the full read go through a resize for that reason.

While recognise() runs on the scanner runtime, runCapture converts the photo into a Skia image in parallel (Promise.all). The transition needs the photo to draw. It also saves a copy of the upright photo, but only in a development build, or while the setting "Help improve the app" is on (see the last section).

Flattening the grid: the homography

The problem. You hold the phone at an angle. The square grid looks like a trapezoid in the photo. The model learned from cells that look like straight squares. We must undo the tilt.

Analogy: a picture on a rubber sheet Imagine the photo printed on a stretchy rubber sheet. Pin the four corners of the grid to the four corners of a square frame on the table. Pull. The rubber stretches so the grid becomes a perfect square. Every point of the picture moves a different amount. That stretch, written down as numbers, is a homography.

What the maths says

Simple moves, such as shift, rotate and scale, keep parallel lines parallel. A tilted view does not: the far edge looks shorter, and parallel lines meet at a point in the distance. A homography adds exactly that. It is a 3 × 3 table of numbers. To move a point (x, y), treat it as (x, y, 1), multiply by the table, and then divide the first two results by the third: 

x' = (a·x + b·y + c) / (g·x + h·y + 1)
y' = (d·x + e·y + f) / (g·x + h·y + 1)

The divisor is what makes distant things smaller. If g and h are zero, there is no division, and you get the simple moves. There are eight numbers (the ninth is fixed at 1). Four corner pairs give exactly eight equations, which is why four points are enough.

          A homography is exact for a flat sheet. A page in a thick book curves near the spine, and straight lines of the grid become bent. One homography cannot fix a bend. The plan in ADR-004 is to find the 100 line crossings and flatten each cell from its own four corners.
          That is not built yet (see the callout at the top). Today, the cells near a spine may be a little off, and the model must cope. The "review this cell" rings on the correction screen exist partly for this reason.

The corners always come from the photo. The locked quad picks the grid nearest to it (within half a grid side), so a neighbouring sudoku is not taken by mistake.

        


        
        
Cutting 81 cells and reading them

        
Cut

        

          After the warp, the grid is a 576 × 576 square. FLAT_SIZE = 9 * CELL_SIZE = 9 × 64. Cutting is plain arithmetic. Cell number n starts at
          row rowOf(n) * 64 and column columnOf(n) * 64. sliceCells in mobile/src/scanner/pixels.ts copies 64 rows of 64 pixels for each cell.
          The result is one array: 81 × 64 × 64 × 3 = 995,328 bytes, cell after cell, row by row. No OpenCV is needed.
        

        

        
Prepare: the model contract

        

          The model is trained in Python. The app runs it in TypeScript. The two sides never import each other (SUD-07). The only thing that keeps them in agreement is a written promise, the
          model contract. A mismatch, such as inverted colours, does not crash anything. It silently makes every scan wrong. So the contract fixes the input exactly:
        

        

          
StepRuleWhy

          
            
1. Grey0.299 R + 0.587 G + 0.114 BColour carries no digit information. These weights match how the eye sees brightness

            
2. CropNone. The model reads the whole 64 × 64ADR-020: pen digits can reach the cell border. A crop cut 14 to 35 % of the ink of the misread ones

            
3. Resize64 × 64 to 32 × 32, mean of each 2 × 2 blockA smaller input makes a smaller, faster model. The exact definition is written as a matrix product, so Python and TypeScript agree

            
4. Polarityx = 1 - grey / 255Ink is bright (near 1) and paper is dark (near 0). Most pixels are paper, so most inputs are 0

            
5. Shape[81, 32, 32, 1], float32One batch with the 81 cells of one grid

          
        

        

          preprocessCells in mobile/src/scanner/preprocess.ts implements it in plain TypeScript. It does not use OpenCV, so a different OpenCV build cannot change the answer.
          Both sides are tested against the same reference pairs in shared/fixtures/preprocess/, and every value must match within 1/255.
        

        

          
Lab: the 2 × 2 average, in miniature

          
The real step shrinks 64 × 64 to 32 × 32. This lab shrinks 8 × 8 to 4 × 4 with the same rule. Click pixels on the left to draw ink. On the right, each number is the average of one 2 × 2 block, after the flip 1 - grey/255.

          

            
INPUT 8 × 8 (click to toggle ink)

            
OUTPUT 4 × 4 (model input)

          

          

          
An ink pixel counts as grey 0 and paper as grey 255, so ink gives 1.0 and paper gives 0.0. A block that is half ink gives 0.5. The border of a digit therefore becomes soft grey, and that is useful for the model: it sees where a stroke begins and ends, not only on or off.

        


        
The model: one call, two heads

        

          The model takes all 81 cells in one batch and answers two questions for each cell. These are the two heads:
        

        

          
HeadShapeClasses, in this orderQuestion

          
            
digit[81, 10]empty, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9Which number is in the cell?

            
kind[81, 4]none, printed, handwritten, notesWhat kind of mark is it?

          
        

        

          Why two heads? Because the game cares about both. A printed 5 is a given: part of the puzzle, locked. A handwritten 5 is an entry: the previous player's guess, which may be wrong. Pencil notes are not a digit. Each head ends in a softmax, so its 10 or 4 numbers add up to 1 and read as probabilities.
        

        
decodeOutputs in mobile/src/scanner/classifier.ts turns the raw numbers into one CellResult per cell:

        
    
          
  • digit = the index of the largest probability in the digit head. 0 means empty.
    • 
          
  • kind = the largest of the kind head, as a word.
    • 
          
  • validPair = the two answers make sense together. Empty goes with none or notes. A digit goes with printed or handwritten.
    • 
          
  • confidence = the smaller of the two top probabilities.
    • 
          
  • second = the second-best digit, the guess the correction screen shows when the first is wrong.
    • 
        

        

          Why the smaller? A chain is as strong as its weakest link. If the model is 99 % sure of the digit but 55 % sure of the kind, the cell is doubtful, and the player should check it. Use the lab to feel it.

Loading the model, and not trusting it blindly

        

          loadClassifier (mobile/src/scanner/model.ts) loads cell-classifier.tflite with react-native-fast-tflite on the CPU. The Metal GPU delegate does not work in version 3, and the model is small, so the CPU is fast enough: the model call takes 29 to 51 ms for all 81 cells.
          Before it runs a single scan, the app checks the model three ways:
        

        
    
          
  1. checkManifest reads manifest.json. The contract major version must be one the scanner implements (SUPPORTED_CONTRACTS = [2]). A model trained on cropped cells has contract 1 and is refused (SUD-09).
    2. 
          
  2. createClassifier checks the tensor shapes: input [81, 32, 32, 1] float32, outputs [81, 10] and [81, 4]. Outputs are found by index, which the manifest records, because names are not reliable on the phone.
    3. 
          
  3. A development build also runs a parity check (parity.ts). It feeds 81 reference cells to the phone model and compares the answers with what PyTorch gave on the laptop.
    4. 
        

        
The model file is not in git
          The .tflite file is private. A fresh clone does not bundle until the developer runs just install-model. Chapter 5 explains how a model gets from training to the phone (just promote-model, SUD-06).
        


        

          
The digit head is 99 % sure a cell holds a "4". The kind head is 60 % sure it is "handwritten". What is the cell's confidence, and what happens on the correction screen?

          

            
            
            
            
          

          
Confidence is the lower of the two top probabilities. A doubt in either head is a doubt about the cell. Because 0.60 is under the threshold 0.8, cellLook marks it uncertain and the screen draws a ring.

The transition and the overlay effects

        

          The problem. Between lock-on and the correction screen, a recognition takes around half a second. A frozen screen would feel broken. A smooth animation hides the wait and tells the story: "I saw your grid, I flattened it, I read the numbers."
          This is the most polished part of the app. The design is in the scan-pipeline concept (section "The transition") and the effects in
          ADR-014.
        

        
A clock with two gates

        

          The animation is not a fixed timeline. One transition clock on the UI thread drives every layer. The clock can wait at a gate, until the thing it needs is ready:
        

        

          
GateAtOpens whenWhile it is closed

          
            
Photo (GATE_PHOTO)0.05 sThe photo is a Skia imageThe camera preview stays visible

            
Results (GATE_RESULTS)0.70 srecognise() returns okA spinner shows after 1 s. On failure the gate stays closed and a failure panel slides up

          
        

        

          Digits cannot appear before they are known. If recognise() is slow, the clock holds at 0.70 s. When the results arrive, the digit sweep, the rings and the panel shift later by the wait
          (sweepShift), so no digit "pops in". If recognition is fast, the clock never waits. The animation takes 1.5 s when nothing waits.
          The pure functions are in mobile/src/scan/utils/transition.utils.ts. They are easy to test because they take numbers and return numbers.

The layers, bottom to top

        
    
          
  1. Camera preview. It stops when the photo is drawn.
    2. 
          
  2. Transition canvas (TransitionCanvas). A light background, the photo warped from the locked quad towards the square, the white cells and the growing lines.
    3. 
          
  3. Overlay, capture phase. The gathered dots ride the same moving quad. They pulse, turn to ink and shrink into the line crossings.
    4. 
          
  4. Correction screen views. Title, digits, rings and panel. They read the same clock.
    5. 
          
  5. Flash. A white flash at the start.
    6. 
        

        

          The photo layer uses photoMatrix: quadToQuad from the locked quad in photo pixels to the moving screen quad. It is the same homography you built in the lab above, but animated: frameAt uses
          lerpQuad(from, to, flatten) to slide the four corners from the locked grid to the final square, and the matrix follows. The last frame equals the correction screen, so the hand-over is invisible.
          boardLinesMatrix and insetUnit keep that last frame exact to the pixel.
        

        
Who owns what
          The capture session (useCaptureSession) owns the state from lock-on to the review. Four small files hold its parts: captureAttempt.ts (a pure reducer, the cross-fade rule and the gates),
          captureRun.ts (the capture and import pipelines), useTransitionClock (the clock on the UI thread) and transition.utils.ts (frame maths). A stale attempt, for example after the screen unmounted, writes no state and disposes any image that arrives late.
        


        
The overlay effects (ADR-014)

        

          While the player aims, the screen shows an effect: the outline, dots, or ripples. The default is Gathering dots. The prototype had 21 effects, and the developer judges them on a real camera.
          So the code makes an effect a plug-in module. Click a box to see how the pieces fit.
        

        

          
          
Click a box to read about it.

          
The smoothed quad
The engine receives the outline in view points, from smoothQuad and toPreview. The preview crops the frame to fill the screen ('cover' mapping), so toPreview converts 0..1 frame coordinates into view points.

          
stepScene: the facts, once
mobile/src/scan/effects/scene.ts. It computes for every frame: the grid and its matrices m and inverse (from unitToQuad), the events found and lost, how present and how much in motion the grid is. Every effect reads the same Scene. No effect repeats this work, and no effect may change the scene.

          
trackLock: the rule lives elsewhere
The lock-on tracker runs right after stepScene. It gives back only two numbers to the scene: lockProgress and lockedAt. An effect never sees the rules of ADR-019. It only draws "how close to locked", for example dots that settle and get brighter. From lock-on, the scene keeps the locked quad and ignores new detections.

          
An effect is three worklets
create(scene) makes the state. step(state, scene) moves it. draw(canvas, state, scene, kit) paints. That is the whole Effect type in mobile/src/scan/effects/types.ts. A shader effect (ripples, grid-lattice) adds one source string in SkSL, which the engine compiles once. To add an effect: add a folder and one line in effects/registry.ts.

            
Gathering dots makes a swarm of free dots. When a grid appears, each free dot flies on a curved path (FLIGHT = 0.7 s, staggered by up to 0.35 s) to one of the 100 line crossings. Lock-on progress brightens them. In the capture phase they pulse, turn to ink and shrink into the crossings of the drawn lines. The matching of dots to crossings is assignNearest.

        

        

          The budget is 4 ms per frame on the UI thread for the scene and one effect. A development build shows a dev pill that lists the effects and switches at once. A release build has no pill, reads no setting, and runs the default effect only.
        

        

        
        
The correction screen and the correction session

        

          The problem. The model is very good but not perfect. A misread digit in a sudoku makes the puzzle unsolvable. A scanner that cannot be corrected is a toy.
        

        
Analogy: a proofreader with a pencil
          A proofreader does not re-type the page. They circle the words they doubt and let the author decide. The correction screen circles the cells the model doubts, and lets the player confirm or fix them in a few taps.
        

        
From a model answer to what the player sees

        
cellLook in mobile/src/scan/utils/correction.utils.ts turns a CellResult into a CellLook:

        

          
Model saidLook (style)Loads in the game as

          
            
digit, kind printedgivenA given (locked)

            
digit, kind handwrittenentry (blue)A player entry. It stays even if it conflicts with the solution

            
empty, kind notesnotesEmpty. Reading pencil notes is out of scope

            
empty, any other kindemptyEmpty

          
        

        

          A look is uncertain when confidence < REVIEW_CONFIDENCE (0.8) or the digit and kind do not fit (!validPair). An uncertain cell gets a ring. The threshold is provisional: the aim is "about 3 rings or fewer on a normal scan".
          A development slider tunes it.
        

        
Edits sit on top of the result

        

          The edits never change the scanner result. useCorrectionEdits keeps a small record, CorrectionEdits: overrides (cells the player changed), checked (cells the player confirmed with Yes), selected and picking.
          The screen shows editedLooks(base, edits): for each cell, the override if it exists, else the original look with the ring removed if the player checked it. All the edit functions in
          mobile/src/scan/utils/correctionEdit.utils.ts are pure: old edits in, new edits out. That makes them easy to test. It also makes Retake trivial, because there is nothing to undo.
        

        
        

          Three small rules make the flow feel good. A digit typed into a cell that had none becomes a given, because a printed digit the model missed is the common case. An answer on a doubtful cell jumps to the next doubtful cell, in reading order. And the player can switch a cell between
          given and entry (setKind), which matters because the solver will treat them differently.
        

        
The play gate

        

          "Play this puzzle" only turns on when the puzzle is playable. checkPlay in mobile/src/scan/utils/play.utils.ts uses the solver from
          @sudoku/core (see chapter 2). Only the givens go to the solver. An entry is the player's guess, not a clue.
        

        

          
SituationBlocks play?The screen says

          
            
Uncertain cellsNo: the ring is a hintNothing more

          The solver runs only when the panel shows the summary, never on each key press and never during the transition. A scan that passes loads with the source scanned, and the level is graded from the techniques the solver needs.

Two sessions, two jobs

        

          Two hooks own the state of this part of the app. They have different jobs.
        

        

          
Capture session useCaptureSession
From lock-on to the review. Holds the attempt, the gates, the clock, the plan and the photo image. It owns starting the scan.

The correction screen. It reads the capture session for the phase and the result. It combines the edits, the verdict (useVerify) and the solved pulse. It owns fixing the scan.

        

        

          The screen only draws. It sends intents ("select cell 12", "set digit 4") to the session.
